## 1. Core Competence Analysis

### Core Questions Addressed:
1. In the transfer process, why does a business need to be valued?
2. What factors affect the value of the company?
3. How does the perspective of the buyer and seller differ in valuation?

### Answers & Synthesis:

* **1. Why Value a Business:**  
  A business valuation is needed for legal, tax, and commercial reasons [FEE, 2001; Liljeström et al., 2024; Wideman, 2024]. Commercial banks strictly require a formal valuation before approving acquisition loans or guarantees [FEE, 2001; Liljeström et al., 2024]. Tax authorities need it to calculate capital gains, gift, or inheritance taxes [FEE, 2001]. It also sets an objective price baseline for purchase negotiations and ensures fair financial division for non-active family heirs [FEE, 2001; Liljeström et al., 2024].

* **2. Factors Affecting Company Value:**  
  Company value depends on both quantitative financial data and qualitative operational factors [Liljeström et al., 2024; Parker, 2020]. Quantitative drivers include historical revenue, profit margins, EBITDA, working capital, and net asset value [FEE, 2001; Parker, 2020]. Qualitative drivers include brand reputation, customer concentration, and proprietary technology [FEE, 2001; Liljeström et al., 2024]. Management autonomy is critical: if a business relies entirely on the founder, it carries high transferability risk and trades at low multiples (\\(1\times–2\times\\) SDE) [Parker, 2020]. Firms with autonomous management command higher multiples (\\(3\times–5\times\\) SDE) [Parker, 2020].

* **3. Buyer vs. Seller Perspective Standoff:**  
  Sellers and buyers view valuation differently, creating a negotiation standoff [Parker, 2020; Standaert et al., 2024]. Sellers look backward with emotional attachment, overvaluing their years of hard work, sweat equity, and personal identity [Liljeström et al., 2024; Parker, 2020]. Buyers look forward with a risk-averse mindset, focusing strictly on future cash flows, debt serviceability, and transition risks [Parker, 2020; Sefiane, 2024]. In family businesses, owners also value Socio-Emotional Wealth (SEW), prioritizing family legacy, reputation, and control over pure profit [Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007; Liljeström et al., 2024]. Objective valuation approaches (Asset, Market, and Income/DCF methods) and normalized profit add-backs are necessary to bridge this gap [FEE, 2001; Parker, 2020].

## 3. Personal Learning Takeaway

Studying Module 2 and working on company valuation was one of the most practical parts of this course for me. Before this module, I thought valuation was just a simple mathematical calculation based on accounting balance sheets. Learning how subjective and emotional valuation really is in small and family-owned businesses was a major eye-opener.

I found the concept of Seller’s Discretionary Earnings (SDE) and profit add-backs especially interesting. It was fascinating to see how small business tax records hide true earning power, and how financial advisors must add back owner salaries and personal perks to find the real profit. Understanding the psychological standoff between emotional sellers (who count their years of sweat equity) and risk-averse buyers (who focus on return on investment) showed me why deals often stall. Furthermore, learning about Socio-Emotional Wealth (SEW) helped me understand why family business owners often accept price discounts or prioritize legacy over maximizing cash. This topic deepened my interest in corporate finance and M&A advisory, and I definitely want to explore business valuation further in my future studies.

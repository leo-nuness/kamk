# Module 1: Awareness Raising and Family Business

## Core Competence Analysis

### Topic 1.3: Definition, Characteristics, and the Three-Circle Model of Family Business

* **Core Question / Competence Addressed:**
  1. What is the definition and what are the core characteristics of a family business?
  2. How does the Three-Circle Model define the interactions between family, ownership, and business?
  3. How do the overlapping sectors generate organizational conflict, and why is structured communication essential for continuity?

* **Theoretical Foundations & Literature Synthesis:**
  Family businesses represent the predominant corporate form in European economies, yet they operate under distinct governance challenges (European Commission, 2009):
  * **Definition & Characteristics:** There is no single legal definition of a family business. However, it is generally defined as an enterprise where two or more family members are involved and hold majority ownership or decisive voting control. Typically, the founder serves as both owner and operating leader, family members work as employees, and the firm intends to transition control from one generation to the next.
  * **The Three-Circle Model:** Tagiuri and Davis (1996) explain the structural complexity of family firms using three overlapping subsystems:
    * *Family:* Governed by emotions, unconditional support, and equality.
    * *Ownership:* Governed by equity rights, capital preservation, and return on investment.
    * *Business:* Governed by commercial performance, productivity, and meritocracy.
    The overlap between these circles creates seven distinct stakeholder groups (e.g., family members who are neither owners nor employees; non-family operating employees; family owner-managers).
  * **Inter-Sector Friction & Solutions:** Because each circle pursues conflicting goals (family equality vs. business competence vs. shareholder dividends), friction is constant. Practical solutions require clear governance instruments, such as Family Constitutions, independent advisory boards, clear employment criteria, and formal communication channels to keep emotional issues separate from business operations.

* **Critical Analysis & Real-World Application:**
  Real-world family business breakdowns rarely happen due to market competition; they happen when the boundaries of the Three-Circle Model collapse. A classic friction point occurs between non-working family owners (who view the business purely as an investment and demand high dividend payouts) and family managers (who want to retain earnings to reinvest in working capital and equipment). Another common risk is employing family members without objective performance standards, which inflates payroll costs and disincentivizes skilled non-family staff. Preventing these operational risks requires establishing formal governance protocols. Family meetings must be separated from executive board decisions, and dividend policies must be linked directly to EBITDA performance and cash flow safety margins rather than personal family spending needs.

* **Personal Learning Reflection:**
  This topic reinforces that in family enterprises, accounting statements and reported EBITDA cannot be analyzed without understanding governance dynamics. Distorted metrics often stem from related-party transactions, unearned family salaries, or irregular dividend extraction. For a credit analyst or corporate advisor, assessing a family business requires evaluating not only solvency and leverage ratios, but also the health of stakeholder dynamics across the Three-Circle Model. Without clear communication structures and formal governance, family disputes directly threaten debt service capacity and business continuity.

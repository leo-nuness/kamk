## Core Competence Analysis

### Topic 4.3: Case Study of a Successful Internal Family Succession – The LEGO Group (Group 3)

* **Core Question / Competence Addressed:**
  1. How did the internal family succession process unfold across four generations of the Kirk Kristiansen family at the LEGO Group?
  2. What were the key financial phases, strategic crises, and operational turning points across the company's history?
  3. How were core family values, quality standards, and innovation transferred across generations?
  4. What governance mechanisms and performance indicators explain why LEGO's succession is deemed successful?

* **Theoretical Foundations & Literature Synthesis:**
  The LEGO Group represents one of Europe's most prominent multi-generational family business successions, demonstrating how long-term family stewardship can be combined with professional corporate governance (Jókai-Szilágyi, Le, & Wilson, 2021):
  * **Multi-Generational Succession Process (1st to 4th Generation):**
    * *1st Generation – Ole Kirk Kristiansen (1932–1958):* Founded LEGO on August 10, 1932, in Billund, Denmark, making wooden toys after the 1929 Wall Street crash ruined his carpentry business. His 17-year-old son, Godtfred, began working alongside him in 1937, creating a 20-year father-son overlap. Despite a 1942 factory fire that destroyed his workshop, Ole rebuilt and invested heavily in new technology: purchasing a German milling machine in the mid-1930s (for 3,000 DKK, equal to one-third of prior-year profits) and a plastic injection molding machine in 1946, which led to the first plastic bricks in 1949.
    * *2nd Generation – Godtfred Kirk Christiansen (1958–1979):* Took over leadership upon Ole's death in 1958 (serving as managing director from 1937 to 1973 and family head until 1995). Godtfred developed the *LEGO System in Play* product architecture, weathered a second woodworking factory fire in 1960 by pivoting fully to plastic, and expanded exports into Germany, Sweden, and Norway.
    * *3rd Generation – Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen (1979–2004):* Grandson of Ole, Kjeld became CEO in 1979. As the first family member with a formal business degree (MBA from IMD Business School, Switzerland), he introduced the *"system within system"* product model, spearheaded international expansion (factories/stores in Germany, the UK, Switzerland, Canada, and Brazil), and initiated digital product lines.
    * *Non-Family Turnaround Transition – Jørgen Vig Knudstorp (2004):* When LEGO faced near-bankruptcy in 2004, Kjeld stepped down as CEO and appointed Jørgen Vig Knudstorp—the first non-family CEO—separating operational management from family ownership.
    * *4th Generation – Thomas Kirk Kristiansen (2007–Present):* Great-grandson of Ole, Thomas joined the company in 2007 and became Deputy Chairman of the LEGO Brand Group in 2016, assuming the 4th-generation leadership mantle after a structured 9-year preparation phase.
  * **Financial Eras & Strategic Restructuring:**
    * *Expansion Era (1970–1991):* Steady financial growth propelled LEGO into the world's top ten toy manufacturers (and the only European firm on the list).
    * *Era of Decline (1992–2004):* Profits eroded as LEGO over-diversified into theme parks and video games (*LEGO Interactive*) while retiring veteran toy designers in 1995–1996 in favor of 30 external innovators who lacked core brick knowledge. LEGO posted its first-ever loss of €26.4 million in 1998 (laying off 1,000 workers) and neared bankruptcy by 2004.
    * *Recovery & Global Leadership (2005–Present):* Under Knudstorp's survival plan, LEGO refocused on its core brick system, sold four LEGOLAND parks to Merlin Entertainments, shut down LEGO Interactive, sold its Swiss factory, and relocated manufacturing to Mexico and distribution from Billund to the Czech Republic. LEGO returned to profit in 2005, expanded into Hungary, Mexico, and China in the 2010s, and adapted to digital competition (adjusting headcount by 1,400 in 2017 to stay agile).
  * **Cultural Transmission ("The Duck Story") & Core Values:**
    * Founder Ole embedded the motto *"Only the best is good enough."* In the famous "Duck Story," young Godtfred bragged about saving money by applying two coats of varnish instead of three to wooden ducks for the Danish Co-op; Ole ordered him to retrieve the shipment from the train station at night and apply the third coat alone. This transferred an uncompromising commitment to quality, customer honesty, bold capital reinvestment, and resilience across four generations, alongside core brand values of *quality, fun, imagination, creativity, caring, and learning*.
  * **Governance Model & Performance Indicators of Success:**
    * *The "Most Active Owner" Governance Model:* To maintain unified governance across a growing family tree, the Kirk Kristiansen family decided that in each generation, one person serves as the *"Most Active Owner"* (Thomas in the 4th generation). This person represents the entire family closely across the *LEGO Group*, *KIRKBI* (the family holding and investment arm), the *LEGO Foundation*, and *LEGO Education*, while all other family shareholders act as engaged, responsible owners and cultural ambassadors (Jókai-Szilágyi et al., 2021).
    * *Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):* Today, LEGO operates 42 offices, 184 retail stores, and employs nearly 19,000 people worldwide. In 2012, LEGO overtook Mattel to become the world's most valuable toy company with a valuation exceeding $\$14.6$ billion, and in May 2018 ranked 97th on the *Forbes* Top 100 World's Most Valuable Brands.

* **Critical Analysis & Real-World Application:**
  The LEGO case study illustrates how a family enterprise can overcome the classic "third-generation curse" through governance adaptability and financial realism. During the 1992–2004 crisis, LEGO suffered from classic corporate overextension: diluting its core product identity while tying up massive capital in low-margin theme parks and software divisions. In many family businesses, an incumbent owner-manager acts as a "monarch" (Sonnenfeld's typology), refusing to cede control until the bank forces liquidation. Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen's decision in 2004 to step down as CEO and hire an external turnaround specialist (Jørgen Vig Knudstorp) was the decisive turning point. By separating operational management from family ownership (held via KIRKBI), LEGO executed painful but necessary balance-sheet restructuring—divesting the LEGOLAND parks to Merlin Entertainments and optimizing manufacturing footprints—without losing family equity control. Furthermore, the *"Most Active Owner"* governance structure prevents the shareholder fragmentation and cousin rivalries that typically paralyze fourth-generation family dynasties.

* **Personal Learning Reflection:**
  Analyzing LEGO's four-generation journey fundamentally reshapes my view of what constitutes a "successful" family succession. From a corporate finance and governance perspective, keeping a business in the family does not mean a family member must always occupy the operational CEO seat. When a family firm reaches global scale or encounters a severe liquidity crisis, bringing in an objective, non-family CEO can save the enterprise while the family transitions into strategic board governance and holding-company stewardship (KIRKBI). Ultimately, LEGO's longevity proves that combining codified cultural values (*"Only the best is good enough"*) with a formal ownership charter (*"Most Active Owner"*) protects both brand equity and multi-generational financial compounding.

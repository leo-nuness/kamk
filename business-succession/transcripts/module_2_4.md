## 2. Structured Summary

### Core Purpose & Context

The presentation explores the fundamentals of company valuation in the context of business transitions and ownership succession. Valuation is driven by three broad mandates: statutory/legal obligations, contractual agreements (e.g., resolving partner disputes or inheritance division), and commercial transactions (e.g., mergers, acquisitions, equity financing, or MBOs).

### The Subjectivity of Family Business Valuation

Valuing family enterprises involves unique complexities because owners systematically perceive greater value in the enterprise than external markets recognize:

* **Emotional Capital:** Owners heavily factor in years of sweat equity, time, and personal identity, often causing objective financial logic to be disregarded during negotiations.


* **Intangible Assets:** Value is driven by reputation across multiple stakeholders (customers, suppliers, competitors), organizational culture, owner motivation, and tacit firm-level knowledge.


* **Key-Person Risk:** When operational success and key customer relationships are concentrated in the owner/CEO, transferring ownership presents substantial continuity risk, which dampens valuation.


* **Succession Utility:** A valuation provides clarity on whether to transfer internally, sell externally, or liquidate, while formalizing a succession plan increases attractiveness to buyers and capital providers.



### The Three Valuation Methodologies

```
                     ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
                     │   Business Valuation Approaches   │
                     └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
                                       │
        ┌──────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                              ▼                              ▼
┌─────────────────┐          ┌───────────────────┐          ┌───────────────────┐
│ Asset Approach  │          │  Market Approach  │          │  Income Approach  │
│ (Cost/Balance)  │          │  (Comparables)    │          │  (Profit × Mult.) │
└─────────────────┘          └───────────────────┘          └───────────────────┘

```

1. **Asset Approach (Cost/Substitution):**
* Computes net asset value by subtracting total liabilities from total assets on the balance sheet.


* Grounded in the economic principle of substitution: *What would it cost to recreate this business to yield equivalent utility?*

* **Limitation:** Fails to capture intangible assets (goodwill, brand loyalty, reputation) effectively.




2. **Market Approach (Comparables):**
* Estimates value by comparing recent transaction multiples of peer firms within the same sector and geography (e.g., local pizzerias).


* **Limitation:** Highly risky because no two private enterprises share identical cost structures, margins, or customer loyalty; prior comps may reflect distressed or mispriced transactions.




3. **Income Approach (Earnings Capitalization):**
* The most common methodology in small business transactions, represented by:

$$\text{Selling Price} = \text{Adjusted Profit (SDE/EBITDA)} \times \text{Multiple}$$



* **Add-backs (Normalizing Earnings):** Legitimate owner benefits, one-time non-recurring expenses, and discretionary personal expenditures are added back to reported earnings only if they were actually expensed and will not recur under new ownership.


* **Multiples Range:** The multiple reflects transferability risk. Owner-centric services (e.g., sole hairdressers) trade at compressed multiples ($1\times - 2\times$), whereas firms with clean books, transferable management, and stable recurring revenue trade higher ($3\times - 5\times$). (U.S. benchmark via BizBuySell averaged $2.31\times$).





### Determinants of Enterprise Value

* **Business Story & History:** Operating longevity, historical revenue trajectories, firm scale, and the underlying motivation for selling (e.g., urgent health issues may force discounted sales).


* **Asset Base (Tangible vs. Intangible):** Capital assets (machinery, real estate, inventory) provide a solid valuation floor, whereas heavy reliance on intangible intellectual property or CEO talent elevates discount rates.


* **Industry & Macro Conditions:** Sector demand-supply balance, cyclical timing (selling in a boom vs. a downturn), and structural industry growth.


* **Customer Dynamics:** Growth traction and customer concentration. High revenue concentration in a few accounts introduces downside risk unless mitigated by exclusive supplier agreements or long-term contracts.


* **Core Financials:** Operating margins and EBIT; businesses generating superior gross margins command premium valuations due to structural pricing power and differentiated operations.


* **Future Financing & Dilution:** The volume of follow-on capital required prior to reaching positive cash flow or exit, ensuring founders and key staff retain adequate equity incentives.



### The Buyer vs. Seller Dynamic

* **Sellers:** Anchor heavily on past emotional investment and effort, requiring empirical justification (clean records, normalized earnings) rather than arbitrary asking prices to substantiate value during deal negotiations.


* **Buyers:** Maintain a quantitative, risk-adjusted posture focused on return on invested capital (ROI), debt serviceability, and operational feasibility under new management. Buyers must formulate a comprehensive acquisition and integration plan to realize projected investment returns.
